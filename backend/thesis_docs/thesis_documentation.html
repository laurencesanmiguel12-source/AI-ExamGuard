1. Introduction and Project Overview

AI ExamGuard is a full-stack online examination platform with integrated AI-based proctoring, built for Arellano University. It allows instructors to create and manage examinations, students to take them remotely under automated monitoring, and administrators to oversee the system as a whole. Unlike many commercial proctoring products, which operate as opaque, vendor-controlled services, every detection mechanism in this system is implemented, trained, and evaluated in-house, using real measured data rather than vendor claims.

The system evolved through a deliberate, incremental methodology: rather than building the full originally-envisioned AI stack at once, each capability (behavioral proctoring, face verification, object detection, and finally a trained composite risk model) was added as its own validated, independently-tested slice. This documentation describes the system as actually implemented and verified, not as originally conceived in the design mockup, and is intended to support the systems requirements, architecture, and methodology chapters of the accompanying undergraduate thesis.

3. System Architecture

The system follows a three-tier architecture: a React single-page application (with a companion browser extension) as the client, a FastAPI REST backend enforcing authentication and business logic, and a PostgreSQL database. AI monitoring runs as backend services invoked synchronously during an exam session rather than as a separate microservice, keeping the deployment simple while each model remains independently swappable.

Figure 1. System architecture: client tier, API tier, AI services, storage, and containerized deployment.

3.1 Backend Module Structure

The backend is organized by responsibility, following a routes → services → models layering:

  • Routes (17 route modules) define HTTP endpoints and their access-control dependencies.
  • Services contain business logic (grading, risk computation, violation logging, audit logging), independent of the HTTP layer.
  • Models (14 SQLAlchemy models) define the persisted schema.
  • Auth dependencies centralize ownership checks (e.g. require_session_owner_student, require_violation_manage_access) so every route reuses the same verified access-control logic rather than re-implementing it.

6. Artificial Intelligence Components

6.1 Face Detection and Verification

Face detection uses YuNet, a small pretrained ONNX detector chosen for robustness to head rotation and lighting compared to a classical Haar cascade. Face recognition uses OpenCV's LBPH (Local Binary Patterns Histograms) algorithm, trained per-student on their own enrolled images at enrollment time (minimum three valid samples) - no external face-recognition dataset or service is used. Raw enrollment photographs are discarded immediately after training; only the resulting per-student model file is retained.

6.2 Object (Phone/Person) Detection

Object detection runs two YOLOv8 passes per check: the unmodified pretrained yolov8s weights (COCO classes) for person counting, and a separately fine-tuned single-class phone specialist model. If the specialist misses on a whole-frame pass, a secondary pose-based check locates wrist keypoints (YOLOv8-pose) and re-examines the cropped hand regions at a lower confidence threshold, catching phones held at angles a whole-frame pass tends to miss.

6.3 Composite Risk Scoring

The risk score (0-100) blends two kinds of signal deliberately, rather than training a single end-to-end model on everything:

  • Behavioral signals (tab switch, fullscreen exit, copy/paste, right-click, extension-detected AI-tool/search-engine use, identity mismatch) are scored with fixed, hand-set weights over a rolling time window. These stay rule-based because no video ground truth exists to train them against - they are browser events, not something visible in webcam footage.
  • Vision signals (face lost, phone detected, multiple people counts) are scored by a trained LogisticRegression model, since real ground truth for these does exist (see 6.4).

The two scores are summed and capped at 100. The vision model's raw output is recalibrated so a session with zero detected evidence scores exactly 0, rather than an arbitrary intercept-driven baseline.

6.4 Training Methodology and Data

A guiding principle throughout AI development on this project was to train only what has real ground truth behind it, and to leave everything else transparently rule-based rather than fabricate labels. Two real, distinct data sources were used:

  • An external Kaggle-style phone/face dataset (~22,900 images) for general phone-appearance variety.
  • The MSU Online Exam Proctoring (OEP) dataset - real webcam footage of students during exams, with genuine per-second cheat-event ground truth (reading notes, talking to someone, internet use, phone call, phone use). Only cheat codes with a plausible visual correlate (phone use/call) were used as positive labels for the vision-based risk model; the others are structurally invisible to a camera and were excluded rather than mislabeled.

The fine-tuned phone specialist trained on this combined data measured, on the OEP validation split specifically (held out by subject, not by frame, to avoid leaking a person's face/room across the split): precision 0.952, recall 0.828, mAP50 0.917 - compared to 0.328/0.113/0.334 for the original un-fine-tuned model on the same real footage. The risk model achieved AUC 0.879 on held-out validation windows.

Known limitation: real phone-positive ground truth in the OEP dataset comes from only four subjects. This is disclosed explicitly in Section 9 rather than presented as a larger-sample result than it is.

7. Security and Access Control

  • Authentication: JWT bearer tokens; passwords hashed with bcrypt via passlib.
  • Authorization: role-based (Student / Instructor / Admin) plus resource-ownership checks on every session-, violation-, and exam-scoped endpoint - an instructor cannot read or modify another instructor's exam, and a student cannot access another student's session, violations, or evidence.
  • Closed historical gaps (fixed during development, notable for a security-methodology discussion): an early version trusted a client-supplied instructor_id on exam creation, and several session-scoped endpoints (violations, face-check, object-check, answers) initially had no authentication at all. Both were identified via a deliberate security audit and fixed before the corresponding features were considered complete.
  • No public exposure of stored biometric data: face models and violation evidence images are never served through a static file mount; both are only retrievable through authenticated, ownership-checked API routes.
  • Secrets management: the JWT signing secret and database credentials are excluded from version control (.env, gitignored) and were rotated after being found to have been accidentally committed earlier in development.

8. Accessibility and Due-Process Features

Two features address concerns specific to AI-based proctoring's potential for disparate impact and unreviewable accusations:

8.1 Accessibility Accommodations

An administrator can exempt a specific student from face and/or object detection checks, and/or grant extra exam time, with a required documented reason. This is enforced with defense in depth: not only does the client skip the relevant camera/check calls, but the corresponding backend services independently verify the accommodation flag and will never log a violation for an exempted student even if a client-side request is somehow still sent.

8.2 Violation Evidence and Appeals

Vision-based violations (face lost, identity mismatch, phone detected, multiple people) capture the triggering webcam frame as evidence, since these are the only violation types with a webcam frame genuinely available at the moment of detection. A student may contest a violation with a written reason; an instructor or administrator with access to that session reviews the evidence and decision, recording a written resolution (upheld or overturned). This turns an automated flag into a reviewable decision rather than an unappealable accusation.

10. Limitations and Recommendations for Future Work

The following gaps were identified through a structured self-review of the system against what would make it genuinely defensible for university-wide adoption, not just functionally complete:

  1. Fairness/bias audit. No evaluation currently exists of detection performance across different lighting conditions, skin tones, glasses, head coverings, or camera quality. This is the single highest-priority gap, both ethically and as a potential thesis contribution in its own right.
  2. Evaluation methodology rigor. Ground truth for the vision-based risk model currently comes from only four subjects; a frozen holdout (never touched during iterative tuning) and reported confidence intervals or leave-one-subject-out cross-validation would strengthen any generalization claim.
  3. Privacy/data governance documentation. A written retention/deletion policy and a Data Protection Impact Assessment, reviewed against the Philippine Data Privacy Act, are recommended before wider deployment.
  4. Scale and deployment realism. Inference currently runs CPU-side on the backend and has not been load-tested for many concurrent exam sessions. Client-side (in-browser) inference is a promising direction that would address both scale and privacy simultaneously.
  5. A real user study. Even a small pilot (20-30 real students and instructors in an actual exam) surveyed for trust and perceived fairness would materially strengthen the thesis's real-world validation claim beyond offline metrics alone.

These limitations are stated explicitly here, following the same principle applied throughout the project's AI development: an honestly disclosed limitation is preferable to an unstated one, and is itself part of what makes the system's evaluation credible.

11. Conclusion

AI ExamGuard demonstrates a complete, working online examination and AI-proctoring platform in which every automated decision - a face mismatch, a detected phone, a composite risk score - is backed by a real, measured, and disclosed methodology rather than an unverifiable vendor claim. Its accountability features (evidence-backed appeals, accessibility accommodations, and staff audit logging) directly address documented shortcomings of commercial proctoring tools, positioning the system not as a replica of existing products but as a transparent, defensible alternative - provided the limitations in Section 10 are treated as the project's next real milestones rather than left unaddressed.
